Understanding PPO clipping

What the clipped objective rewards, where it stops rewarding, and what it does not guarantee.

Yasaman Samiee · Updated September 17, 2026 · 3 min read

PPO’s clipped objective is easiest to understand by asking one question: how much credit should an update receive for changing the probability of an action that has already been sampled?

Three quantities to keep separate

The probability ratio r = new_probability / old_probability describes the update. The estimated advantage A describes whether the sampled action did better or worse than its baseline. The clipping parameter ε sets a band around a ratio of one.

The contribution being maximized is:

min(r × A, clip(r, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) × A)

This is an objective to maximize. Implementations that minimize a loss typically negate it. Mixing those sign conventions makes the formula much harder to reason about.

A useful action: positive advantage

Take an illustrative old action probability of 0.20, an advantage of +2, and ε = 0.20. If the new probability is 0.30, the ratio is 1.50. The unclipped contribution is 3.00; the clipped contribution is 2.40. The minimum is 2.40.

For this sample, increasing the probability beyond the upper threshold earns no additional objective value while the advantage stays fixed. That does not physically prevent the probability from increasing further.

A poor action: negative advantage

Keep the old probability at 0.20, but use an advantage of −2. Reducing the new probability to 0.10 gives a ratio of 0.50. The two contributions are −1.00 and −1.60, so the objective uses −1.60. The improvement stops receiving extra credit once the ratio falls below 0.80.

The third row matters: increasing the probability of a poor action still receives the worse, unclipped value. The objective does not simply flatten every change outside the band.

What clipping cannot promise

Clipping is not a hard constraint on the whole policy. Parameters are shared across samples, and optimizing a batch can move an individual probability outside the band. The method also cannot repair an inappropriate reward or a poor advantage estimate.

When investigating unstable training, I would inspect approximate KL divergence, the fraction of clipped ratios, entropy, value-function error, and the distribution of returns. A rising training objective alone would not establish better behavior. These are diagnostic suggestions, not evidence of a PPO deployment.

Algorithm reference: Schulman et al., Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms. The numerical examples are worked examples for this explanation.
