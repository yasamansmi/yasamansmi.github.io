Evaluating RAG: retrieval and answers are different problems

A worked evaluation example that separates missing evidence from unsupported answers.

Yasaman Samiee · Updated September 17, 2026 · 3 min read

A retrieval-augmented answer can sound convincing while failing in two different ways: the system may retrieve the wrong evidence, or it may misread the right evidence. A single answer-quality score hides that distinction.

Start with a question whose answer can be checked

Consider a fictional returns assistant. A customer asks: “Can I return a sale item after 20 days?” The current policy says sale items have a 14-day window; full-price items have 30 days. An older policy allowed 30 days for everything.

The test case needs more than a reference answer. Record the relevant policy passage, its effective date, the expected answer, and whether the question is answerable from the available documents. This makes a failure diagnosable.

Check retrieval before changing the prompt

Suppose the retriever returns five passages, including the old policy and several generic returns pages, but misses the current sale-item clause. A more forceful instruction to “be accurate” does not repair that missing evidence.

For a small illustrative evaluation set of 20 questions with one labeled supporting passage each, retrieving that passage for 16 questions gives a hit rate at five of 16/20 = 80%. These are example numbers, not benchmark results. When questions need multiple passages, measure evidence coverage too: finding one clause may not be enough.

Inspect misses by cause: outdated documents, poor chunk boundaries, ambiguous wording, permission filters, or ranking. Change one part of retrieval, then rerun the same cases. A keyword baseline is useful; a vector database is not a requirement for every retrieval system.

Then evaluate the answer against its evidence

Diagnosing the fictional returns example
Retrieved evidenceAnswerWhat to investigate
Current 14-day clause“Yes, within 30 days”Generation contradicts its evidence.
Only the old policy“Yes, within 30 days”Retrieval freshness; a fluent answer is still wrong.
No relevant policy“I cannot confirm from these documents”Appropriate uncertainty; investigate the retrieval miss separately.

Score correctness, support for individual claims, and citation accuracy separately. Include questions the corpus cannot answer. Otherwise a system that always produces an answer may look stronger than one that recognizes missing evidence.

A small evaluation plan

Freeze a representative question set, label supporting evidence, and compare retrieval configurations before tuning generation. Review failures manually, especially conflicting policies and questions requiring multiple facts. Track latency and cost alongside quality. More retrieved text can add irrelevant or contradictory material.

The design principle is to keep evidence availability separate from evidence use. That makes the next engineering step specific: repair the index, change retrieval, or improve how the answer uses the context.

Background: Lewis et al., Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks. The returns scenario and evaluation plan above are illustrative, not results from that paper.
